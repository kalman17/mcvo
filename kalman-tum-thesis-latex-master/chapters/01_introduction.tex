\chapter{Introduction}\label{chapter:introduction}

Recovering three-dimensional scene structure from video is a central problem in computer vision, with applications ranging from novel view synthesis and augmented reality to autonomous navigation and robotics. A prerequisite for virtually all of these tasks is knowledge of the camera geometry: both the intrinsic parameters that describe the camera's internal optics and the extrinsic parameters that describe how the camera moves through space. While controlled laboratory settings can provide these parameters through calibration targets or specialised hardware, the vast majority of available video data---from consumer devices, dashcams, surveillance feeds, and online platforms---is captured with unknown cameras under uncontrolled conditions.

Estimating camera intrinsics and extrinsics from such casual, in-the-wild video presents a significant challenge. The scenes are frequently dynamic, containing moving objects, people, and non-rigid content that violate the static-world assumptions of classical multi-view geometry. Camera motion is arbitrary and unpredictable, and intrinsic parameters vary between devices and recording conditions. Ground-truth annotations for camera parameters are expensive or infeasible to obtain at scale, limiting the applicability of supervised learning approaches.

\section{Motivation and Problem Statement}\label{sec:motivation}

Classical Structure-from-Motion (SfM) systems such as COLMAP~\parencite{schonberger2016structure} estimate camera parameters through iterative feature matching and bundle adjustment. While these methods achieve strong multi-frame consistency through global optimisation, they require test-time iteration, assume a predominantly static scene, and can fail on casual videos with weak texture, dynamic content, or poor camera trajectories. Supervised learning-based approaches~\parencite{wang2024dust3r,yugay2025vot,lin2025depth} replace test-time optimisation with feed-forward inference, improving speed and robustness, but they require ground-truth pose or geometry labels for training. This supervision is expensive to acquire at scale and limits the diversity of training data, constraining generalisation to new domains and camera types.

Self-supervised methods address these limitations by deriving training signals from geometric constraints inherent in image formation itself, rather than from explicit labels. AnyCam~\parencite{wimbauer2025anycam} exemplifies this paradigm: it distils information from frozen pretrained depth and optical flow estimators into a learned pose predictor, training through flow reprojection losses that require no ground-truth annotations. The result is a feed-forward model that is robust to dynamic scenes and can be trained on large-scale, unlabelled video data. Crucially, AnyCam's pose prediction pipeline includes multi-frame reasoning: an 8-layer self-attention mechanism allows all frames in a sequence to attend to each other when predicting relative poses, and a learned sequence token aggregates information across the full sequence for focal length selection.

However, AnyCam's treatment of camera intrinsics remains a significant limitation. Rather than predicting the focal length directly---which the authors found to be unstable during training---AnyCam employs a 32-candidate selection procedure: it evaluates a fixed set of discrete focal length candidates by computing the induced optical flow for each and uses the multi-frame sequence token to select the best candidate. While this selection does leverage multi-frame information (the sequence token attends to all frames), the fundamental constraint is the \emph{discretisation}: the true focal length must be approximated by one of only 32 predefined values. This is both computationally expensive (requiring evaluation of all candidates) and inherently coarse. As preliminary analysis in this work revealed, this procedure can produce substantially inaccurate calibration estimates (Appendix~\ref{chapter:preliminary-results}).

AnyCalib~\parencite{tirado2025anycalib} takes a complementary approach to camera calibration. Rather than selecting from discrete candidates, it regresses dense per-pixel ray directions using a DINOv2 ViT-L/14 backbone and a Light-DPT decoder, then fits a camera model to the predicted rays in closed form via RANSAC. This model-agnostic formulation handles perspective, wide-angle, and fisheye cameras without restricting the lens model. However, AnyCalib processes each frame entirely independently: it has no mechanism for temporal consistency across video sequences, and it does not address camera pose estimation.

The complementary strengths and weaknesses of these two systems motivate the present work. AnyCam provides robust, self-supervised pose estimation with multi-frame reasoning, but its calibration is fundamentally limited by the 32-candidate discretisation. AnyCalib provides accurate, continuous calibration that is not constrained to discrete candidates, but processes each frame independently with no temporal consistency or pose estimation capability. This thesis investigates whether integrating these approaches---replacing AnyCam's discrete candidate system with AnyCalib's continuous calibration pipeline, and enforcing multi-frame calibration consistency---can improve both calibration accuracy and pose estimation quality.

\section{Challenges in Existing Approaches}\label{sec:challenges}

Three key challenges motivate the technical contributions of this thesis:

\paragraph{Discrete and unreliable calibration in self-supervised systems.}
AnyCam's 32-candidate focal length procedure constrains calibration to a fixed set of discrete values, regardless of the true focal length. This discretisation limits accuracy fundamentally: the true value must be approximated by the nearest candidate. Additionally, evaluating all 32 candidates through the flow induction pipeline imposes significant computational overhead. Preliminary experiments conducted in this work confirmed that this procedure can produce calibration estimates with substantial error relative to ground truth (Appendix~\ref{chapter:preliminary-results}).

\paragraph{Per-frame calibration without temporal consistency.}
AnyCalib provides continuous, accurate calibration that is not limited to discrete candidates, but it processes each frame entirely independently. Since a single camera has fixed intrinsic parameters throughout a video sequence, per-frame predictions are unnecessarily noisy and inconsistent. Enforcing consistency across frames should yield more accurate and stable calibration estimates. However, naively computing flow between all $\binom{N}{2}$ frame pairs for multi-frame training scales quadratically; an efficient mechanism is needed.

\paragraph{Disconnection between calibration and pose estimation.}
Existing methods address calibration and pose estimation largely in isolation. AnyCalib produces accurate per-frame calibration but has no awareness of temporal consistency or downstream pose estimation quality. AnyCam handles both tasks but its calibration is constrained by discrete candidates rather than being jointly optimised with pose estimation in a continuous space. As illustrated in Table~\ref{tab:related-work-comparison}, no existing method simultaneously provides label-free training, feed-forward inference, accurate continuous calibration with multi-frame consistency, robustness to dynamic scenes, and model-agnostic intrinsic recovery. Bridging this gap requires an architecture that can jointly reason about calibration and pose across multiple frames.

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes two main contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{AnyCam $\boldsymbol{\times}$ AnyCalib integration.} AnyCam's 32-candidate focal length system is replaced with AnyCalib's direct calibration pipeline. The predicted intrinsics are converted to a focal length embedding and injected into AnyCam's pose head, conditioning pose prediction on accurate calibration rather than coarse discrete candidates. This integration improves both calibration accuracy and downstream pose estimation quality while simplifying the inference pipeline.

    \item \textbf{Multi-Frame Calibration Transformer (MCT).} To enforce calibration consistency across video frames, a transformer module is introduced between AnyCalib's pretrained DINOv2 ViT-L/14 backbone and its Light-DPT decoder. The MCT aggregates spatial features from $N$ frames at four resolution scales via cross-frame self-attention, then mean-pools across frames to produce a single consistent feature representation. This representation is decoded through the frozen AnyCalib pipeline to produce temporally consistent intrinsics. The MCT adds approximately 25 million trainable parameters while all pretrained components remain frozen, yielding a system where only 7.5\% of parameters are trained.
\end{enumerate}

Both contributions are trained end-to-end using self-supervised flow reprojection losses combined with a calibration anchor loss that keeps predictions near AnyCalib's pseudo ground truth. Multi-frame consistency is enforced efficiently through flow composition, which derives long-range flows from consecutive flows via bilinear interpolation, reducing the cost from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N)$ flow computations. A staged training pipeline (pose head initialisation, MCT pre-training, and joint end-to-end optimisation) enables stable convergence. Early validation experiments confirmed that multi-frame consistency improves pose accuracy beyond both the AnyCam baseline and single-frame AnyCalib averaging (Appendix~\ref{chapter:preliminary-results}).

\section{Thesis Outline}\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.

Chapter~\ref{chapter:notation_guide} establishes the mathematical notation and symbol conventions used throughout the thesis. Chapter~\ref{chapter:fundamentals} introduces the geometric foundations---camera projection, depth, optical flow, multi-view consistency, and self-supervised learning---as well as the neural network architectures (Vision Transformers, DPT) that underpin modern geometric vision systems.

Chapter~\ref{chapter:related-work} surveys related work in camera pose and intrinsic estimation, comparing optimisation-based, supervised, and self-supervised approaches as well as standalone helper methods for depth, calibration, and optical flow.

Chapter~\ref{chapter:solution-approach} presents the technical solution: the integration of AnyCalib into the AnyCam pipeline, the flow composition mechanism for multi-frame consistency, the Multi-Frame Calibration Transformer architecture, and the staged training procedure.

Chapter~\ref{chapter:evaluation} describes the evaluation methodology, including metrics for pose accuracy and calibration quality, the datasets used for validation and testing, and the experimental results.

Chapter~\ref{chapter:future-work} discusses directions for future research, and Chapter~\ref{chapter:conclusion} concludes the thesis.
