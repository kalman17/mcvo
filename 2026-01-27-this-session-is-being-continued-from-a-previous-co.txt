  # Fundamentals (First Half)                                               
                                                                            
  This chapter introduces the geometric concepts required to understand     
  camera calibration and camera motion estimation from video. The goal is   
  not to survey computer vision broadly, but to establish a minimal and     
  coherent geometric model of how images are formed, how depth and motion   
  relate to that model, and how these quantities are represented in modern  
  learning-based systems. No prior background in computer vision is         
  assumed; familiarity with linear algebra and basic geometry is            
  sufficient.                                                               
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 1. Camera Geometry and Image Formation                                 
                                                                            
  At its core, a camera is a device that maps points in three-dimensional   
  space to points on a two-dimensional image. This mapping is governed by   
  projective geometry. Understanding this mapping—and how it can be         
  inverted approximately—is the foundation of camera calibration and pose   
  estimation.                                                               
                                                                            
  ### 1.1 The Pinhole Camera Model                                          
                                                                            
  The pinhole camera is the simplest and most widely used mathematical      
  model of image formation. It assumes that all light rays entering the     
  camera pass through a single point, called the *optical centre*, before   
  intersecting an image plane.                                              
                                                                            
  Consider a 3D point (\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^3) expressed in a fixed    
  world coordinate system. To determine where this point appears in an      
  image, three conceptual steps are applied:                                
                                                                            
  1. **Rigid transformation (pose):** The point is expressed in the         
  camera’s coordinate system using a rotation and a translation.            
  2. **Perspective projection:** The 3D point is projected onto a 2D image  
  plane.                                                                    
  3. **Pixel mapping:** The projected point is mapped to discrete pixel     
  coordinates.                                                              
                                                                            
  These steps are combined into a single linear equation using homogeneous  
  coordinates:                                                              
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  s,\tilde{\mathbf{p}} =                                                    
  \mathbf{K},[\mathbf{R}\mid\mathbf{t}],\tilde{\mathbf{X}},                 
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  where (\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = (X,Y,Z,1)^\top) and (\tilde{\mathbf{p}} =     
  (u,v,1)^\top) are homogeneous 3D and 2D coordinates, respectively. The    
  scalar (s>0) corresponds to the depth of the point along the camera’s     
  optical axis.                                                             
                                                                            
  The matrix (\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{K}[\mathbf{R}\mid\mathbf{t}]) is known   
  as the *camera projection matrix*.                                        
                                                                            
  ### 1.2 Camera Intrinsics and Extrinsics                                  
                                                                            
  The projection matrix separates naturally into two components:            
                                                                            
  * **Extrinsic parameters** ((\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t})) describe the *pose* 
  of the camera: its orientation and position in the world.                 
  * **Intrinsic parameters** (\mathbf{K}) describe the internal geometry of 
  the camera sensor.                                                        
                                                                            
  The intrinsic matrix has the standard form                                
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{K} =                                                              
  \begin{pmatrix}                                                           
  f_x & 0 & c_x \                                                           
  0 & f_y & c_y \                                                           
  0 & 0 & 1                                                                 
  \end{pmatrix},                                                            
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  where (f_x, f_y) are the focal lengths measured in pixels, and ((c_x,     
  c_y)) is the principal point, typically close to the image centre.        
  Together, these parameters determine the camera’s field of view and pixel 
  scaling.                                                                  
                                                                            
  The rotation matrix (\mathbf{R} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)) and translation       
  vector (\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^3) define how the camera moves through  
  space. Estimating these quantities from images is known as *camera pose   
  estimation*. When comparing two images in a video sequence, one is        
  usually interested in the *relative pose* between consecutive frames.     
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 2. From Pixels to Rays and Depth                                       
                                                                            
  While projection maps 3D points to pixels, many geometric problems        
  require reasoning in the opposite direction: from pixels back into        
  three-dimensional space.                                                  
                                                                            
  ### 2.1 Pixel Back-Projection and Viewing Rays                            
                                                                            
  Each pixel in an image corresponds not to a single 3D point, but to a     
  *ray* extending from the camera into the scene. This ray represents all   
  points that would project to that pixel.                                  
                                                                            
  Given a pixel (\mathbf{p} = (u,v)), we first remove the effect of camera  
  intrinsics by computing *normalised image coordinates*:                   
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}.                         
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  This defines a direction in the camera coordinate system. Normalising     
  this vector yields a unit ray direction                                   
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{r}(u,v) = \frac{\mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}}{|\mathbf{K}^{- 
  1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}|},                                                   
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  which lies on the unit sphere. Every visible scene point corresponding to 
  pixel ((u,v)) lies somewhere along this ray.                              
                                                                            
  ### 2.2 Depth Maps                                                        
                                                                            
  To recover a specific 3D point along a ray, an additional scalar is       
  required: *depth*. A **depth map** assigns to each pixel a positive       
  scalar (d(u,v)), representing the distance of the visible surface point   
  from the camera along the optical axis.                                   
                                                                            
  Combining a depth map with back-projection lifts the image into 3D:       
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{X}_c(u,v) = d(u,v),\mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}.             
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  Depth maps therefore provide a dense geometric description of the scene   
  from a single viewpoint. Modern systems obtain depth maps from pretrained 
  neural networks. Although depth from a single image is inherently         
  ambiguous up to a global scale, such estimates provide strong relative    
  geometric structure and are sufficient for many motion-related tasks.     
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 3. Ray-Based and Model-Agnostic Camera Representations                 
                                                                            
  The pinhole model assumes an ideal camera. Real cameras often deviate     
  from this model due to lens effects. Instead of explicitly modelling      
  these effects with additional parameters, many modern approaches adopt a  
  more general representation.                                              
                                                                            
  In a **ray-based camera model**, the camera is represented directly by    
  the mapping                                                               
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  (u,v) \mapsto \mathbf{r}(u,v),                                            
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  assigning a 3D ray direction to each pixel. This mapping can be stored as 
  a dense lookup table or predicted by a neural network. Such               
  representations are *model-agnostic*: they do not assume a particular     
  lens type and apply equally to perspective, wide-angle, or fisheye        
  cameras.                                                                  
                                                                            
  Because ray directions lie on the surface of a sphere, care must be taken 
  when predicting them. A common strategy is to parameterise rays using     
  angular coordinates or to predict them in a local tangent plane and map   
  them back to the sphere, ensuring geometric consistency.                  
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 4. Optical Flow as Dense Correspondence                                
                                                                            
  When a camera moves, the same 3D scene point generally appears at         
  different pixel locations in consecutive images. **Optical flow**         
  captures this apparent motion.                                            
                                                                            
  Formally, optical flow is a dense vector field                            
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{w}_{i\to j}(u,v) = (\Delta u, \Delta v),                          
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  which maps each pixel in frame (i) to its corresponding location in frame 
  (j). Optical flow provides pixel-level correspondences across frames and  
  serves as a crucial geometric cue for estimating camera motion.           
                                                                            
  In this thesis, optical flow is treated as an observed quantity obtained  
  from a pretrained model. Given depth and camera motion, one can predict   
  how pixels *should* move between frames. Comparing this predicted motion  
  with observed optical flow yields a powerful self-supervised training     
  signal, which is described in later sections.

  ## 5. Multi-View Geometry and Camera Motion                               
                                                                            
  The concepts introduced so far describe geometry from a single viewpoint. 
  When multiple images of the same scene are available, additional          
  geometric constraints arise from the fact that the scene itself is static 
  while the camera moves. These constraints form the basis of *multi-view   
  geometry*.                                                                
                                                                            
  ### 5.1 Relative Camera Motion                                            
                                                                            
  Consider two images captured at different time steps, indexed by (i) and  
  (j). The camera undergoes a rigid motion between these frames, described  
  by a relative rotation (\mathbf{R}*{i\to j}) and translation              
  (\mathbf{t}*{i\to j}).                                                    
                                                                            
  A 3D point observed in frame (i) at pixel ((u,v)) with depth (d_i(u,v))   
  can be reconstructed in the camera coordinate system of frame (i) as      
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{X}_i = d_i(u,v),\mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}.                
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  Applying the relative motion maps this point into the coordinate system   
  of frame (j):                                                             
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{X}*j = \mathbf{R}*{i\to j},\mathbf{X}*i + \mathbf{t}*{i\to j}.    
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  Projecting (\mathbf{X}_j) back into the image plane of frame (j) yields   
  the pixel location where the same scene point should appear. This         
  forward–backward mapping provides a direct link between depth, camera     
  motion, and pixel correspondences.                                        
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 6. Geometric Consistency Across Views                                  
                                                                            
  Multi-view geometry enables the formulation of *consistency constraints*  
  that must hold if the estimated geometry and motion are correct. These    
  constraints are central to learning-based approaches that do not rely on  
  explicit ground-truth labels.                                             
                                                                            
  ### 6.1 View Synthesis and Reprojection                                   
                                                                            
  Given an image from frame (i), its depth map, and the relative camera     
  motion to frame (j), one can synthesise an approximation of frame (j) by: 
                                                                            
  1. Lifting pixels from frame (i) into 3D using depth                      
  2. Transforming the points using the relative pose                        
  3. Projecting them into the image plane of frame (j)                      
                                                                            
  This process, known as *view synthesis*, produces a warped image          
  (\hat{I}_j). If the depth and pose estimates are accurate, (\hat{I}_j)    
  should closely resemble the true image (I_j).                             
                                                                            
  The discrepancy between the synthesised and observed images is measured   
  using a *photometric error*, typically based on pixel-wise intensity      
  differences or perceptual similarity metrics. Minimising this error       
  encourages geometrically consistent predictions.                          
                                                                            
  ### 6.2 Handling Occlusions and Visibility                                
                                                                            
  Not all pixels can be reliably compared across views. Some scene points   
  may become occluded, move outside the field of view, or violate modelling 
  assumptions such as constant illumination.                                
                                                                            
  To address this, modern systems employ visibility masks, forward–backward 
  consistency checks, or robust loss functions that reduce the influence    
  of unreliable regions. These mechanisms ensure that learning is driven    
  primarily by valid geometric correspondences.

  ## 7. Optical Flow as a Geometric Constraint                              
                                                                            
  While optical flow was previously introduced as an observed               
  correspondence field, it can also be derived from geometry. Given depth   
  and camera motion, the reprojection process implicitly defines a          
  predicted optical flow field.                                             
                                                                            
  Let (\mathbf{p}_i) be a pixel in frame (i) and (\mathbf{p}'_j) its        
  reprojected location in frame (j). The predicted flow is                  
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{w}^{\text{geo}}_{i\to j}(u,v) = \mathbf{p}'_j - \mathbf{p}_i.     
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  Comparing this geometric flow with the observed optical flow provides an  
  additional consistency signal. Agreement indicates that depth and pose    
  are mutually consistent, while disagreement reveals geometric errors.     
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 8. Self-Supervised Learning via Geometric Constraints                  
                                                                            
  The key advantage of the geometric relationships described above is that  
  they do not require explicit ground-truth annotations. Instead,           
  supervision arises directly from the physical structure of image          
  formation.                                                                
                                                                            
  ### 8.1 Learning Objectives                                               
                                                                            
  In a self-supervised framework, neural networks are trained to predict    
  quantities such as depth, camera motion, or ray directions by minimising  
  losses derived from:                                                      
                                                                            
  * Photometric reprojection error between synthesised and observed views   
  * Consistency between geometric and observed optical flow                 
  * Smoothness priors on depth and motion fields                            
                                                                            
  These objectives are grounded in geometry rather than semantic labels,    
  making them broadly applicable across datasets and camera configurations. 
                                                                            
  ### 8.2 Scale Ambiguity and Normalisation                                 
                                                                            
  A fundamental limitation of monocular geometry is *scale ambiguity*:      
  depth and translation can only be recovered up to a common scale factor.  
  In practice, this ambiguity is handled by normalising predicted           
  quantities or fixing scale during training.                               
                                                                            
  Although absolute scale is not recovered, relative depth structure and    
  motion direction are sufficient for tasks such as visual odometry and     
  camera calibration.

  ## 7. Robust Estimation with RANSAC

  When fitting geometric models to noisy data with outliers, standard
  least-squares methods can fail catastrophically. **RANSAC (Random Sample 
  Consensus)** provides a robust alternative widely used in computer vision.

  ### 7.1 The RANSAC Algorithm

  RANSAC fits a model by:
  1. Randomly sampling a minimal subset of data points
  2. Computing a candidate model from this subset
  3. Counting how many data points agree with the model (inliers)
  4. Repeating steps 1-3 many times
  5. Selecting the model with the most inliers

  The final model is refined using all identified inliers, typically via
  least-squares optimization.

  ### 7.2 RANSAC in Camera Calibration

  In camera calibration from predicted rays, RANSAC identifies which ray
  predictions are geometrically consistent with a pinhole camera model. Outliers
   may arise from:
  - Errors in visual feature extraction
  - Violations of model assumptions (e.g., lens distortion)
  - Occlusions or dynamic objects

  RANSAC selects the largest subset of rays that fit a pinhole model within a
  tolerance threshold, then refines the calibration parameters using only these
  inliers.

  ### 7.3 Non-Differentiability Challenge

  RANSAC presents a fundamental challenge for neural network training: it is
  **non-differentiable**. The algorithm involves:
  - Random sampling (no gradients for random selection)
  - Discrete inlier/outlier classification (discontinuous)
  - Argmax over candidates (non-differentiable operation)

  Standard backpropagation cannot flow gradients through RANSAC. This motivates
  alternative training strategies when RANSAC is part of the prediction
  pipeline, which will be discussed in later chapters.

  ### 7.4 Weighted Least Squares Refinement

  After RANSAC identifies inliers, the camera model is refined using weighted
  least squares (WLS). Given a set of observations with reliability weights, WLS
   finds parameters that minimize the weighted sum of squared errors:

  \[
  \mathbf{x}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \|\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - 
  \mathbf{b})\|^2
  \]

  where $\mathbf{W}$ is a diagonal weight matrix (typically 1.0 for inliers, 
  near-zero for outliers), $\mathbf{A}$ encodes the measurement equations, and 
  $\mathbf{b}$ contains observed values.

  The closed-form solution:
  \[
  \mathbf{x}^* = (\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{W}^2 \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{A}^\top
  \mathbf{W}^2 \mathbf{b}
  \]

  is differentiable with respect to $\mathbf{b}$ (even when $\mathbf{W}$ and
  $\mathbf{A}$ are held constant), which enables gradient-based optimization in
  specific configurations.

  ## 8. Optical Flow as a Geometric Constraint

  While optical flow was previously introduced as an observed correspondence
  field, it can also be **predicted from geometry**. Given depth and camera
  motion, one can compute where each pixel *should* move between frames.

  ### 8.1 Induced Flow from Geometry

  Consider two consecutive frames $i$ and $j$. For a pixel $(u,v)$ in frame $i$:

  1. **Lift to 3D** using depth $d_i(u,v)$:
     \[
     \mathbf{X}_i = d_i(u,v) \cdot \mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}
     \]

  2. **Transform to frame $j$**:
     \[
     \mathbf{X}_j = \mathbf{R}_{i\to j}\mathbf{X}_i + \mathbf{t}_{i\to j}
     \]

  3. **Project to frame $j$**:
     \[
     \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_j = \mathbf{K}\mathbf{X}_j
     \]

  4. **Compute flow**:
     \[
     \mathbf{w}_{i\to j}^{\text{geo}}(u,v) = \mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_i
     \]

  This **geometrically induced flow** depends on depth, camera motion, and
  intrinsics.

  ### 8.2 Flow Reprojection Loss

  Comparing geometric flow with observed optical flow (from a pretrained network
   like UniMatch) provides a self-supervised training signal:

  \[
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{flow}} = \|\mathbf{w}_{i\to j}^{\text{geo}} -
  \mathbf{w}_{i\to j}^{\text{obs}}\|
  \]

  When depth and camera parameters are predicted by neural networks, minimizing 
  this loss encourages geometrically consistent predictions without requiring 
  ground-truth labels. Mismatches indicate errors in predicted depth, pose, or 
  intrinsics.

  ### 3.2 Tangent-Space Parameterization

  Ray directions lie on the unit sphere, which poses challenges for neural
  network regression (unbounded tangent vectors, gimbal lock). A common approach
   represents rays in tangent space relative to the optical axis:

  For a ray $\mathbf{r} = (r_x, r_y, r_z)$, the tangent coordinates are:
  \[
  \mathbf{t} = (r_x/r_z, r_y/r_z)
  \]

  These coordinates directly relate to field-of-view angles and can be bounded, 
  regularized, and predicted more stably than full 3D vectors. Conversion back 
  to unit rays uses the exponential map:
  \[
  \mathbf{r} = \frac{(t_x, t_y, 1)}{\|(t_x, t_y, 1)\|}
  \]

  This parameterization is particularly useful when learning ray predictions 
  with neural networks.

  ### 9.3 Neural Network Architectures for Geometry

  fuse multi-scale features to produce dense spatial predictions.

  information is preserved during multi-frame processing.

  # Fundamentals (First Half)                                               
                                                                            
  This chapter introduces the geometric concepts required to understand     
  camera calibration and camera motion estimation from video. The goal is   
  not to survey computer vision broadly, but to establish a minimal and     
  coherent geometric model of how images are formed, how depth and motion   
  relate to that model, and how these quantities are represented in modern  
  learning-based systems. No prior background in computer vision is         
  assumed; familiarity with linear algebra and basic geometry is            
  sufficient.                                                               
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 1. Camera Geometry and Image Formation                                 
                                                                            
  At its core, a camera is a device that maps points in three-dimensional   
  space to points on a two-dimensional image. This mapping is governed by   
  projective geometry. Understanding this mapping—and how it can be         
  inverted approximately—is the foundation of camera calibration and pose   
  estimation.                                                               
                                                                            
  ### 1.1 The Pinhole Camera Model                                          
                                                                            
  The pinhole camera is the simplest and most widely used mathematical      
  model of image formation. It assumes that all light rays entering the     
  camera pass through a single point, called the *optical centre*, before   
  intersecting an image plane.                                              
                                                                            
  Consider a 3D point (\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^3) expressed in a fixed    
  world coordinate system. To determine where this point appears in an      
  image, three conceptual steps are applied:                                
                                                                            
  1. **Rigid transformation (pose):** The point is expressed in the         
  camera’s coordinate system using a rotation and a translation.            
  2. **Perspective projection:** The 3D point is projected onto a 2D image  
  plane.                                                                    
  3. **Pixel mapping:** The projected point is mapped to discrete pixel     
  coordinates.                                                              
                                                                            
  These steps are combined into a single linear equation using homogeneous  
  coordinates:                                                              
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  s,\tilde{\mathbf{p}} =                                                    
  \mathbf{K},[\mathbf{R}\mid\mathbf{t}],\tilde{\mathbf{X}},                 
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  where (\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = (X,Y,Z,1)^\top) and (\tilde{\mathbf{p}} =     
  (u,v,1)^\top) are homogeneous 3D and 2D coordinates, respectively. The    
  scalar (s>0) corresponds to the depth of the point along the camera’s     
  optical axis.                                                             
                                                                            
  The matrix (\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{K}[\mathbf{R}\mid\mathbf{t}]) is known   
  as the *camera projection matrix*.                                        
                                                                            
  ### 1.2 Camera Intrinsics and Extrinsics                                  
                                                                            
  The projection matrix separates naturally into two components:            
                                                                            
  * **Extrinsic parameters** ((\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t})) describe the *pose* 
  of the camera: its orientation and position in the world.                 
  * **Intrinsic parameters** (\mathbf{K}) describe the internal geometry of 
  the camera sensor.                                                        
                                                                            
  The intrinsic matrix has the standard form                                
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{K} =                                                              
  \begin{pmatrix}                                                           
  f_x & 0 & c_x \                                                           
  0 & f_y & c_y \                                                           
  0 & 0 & 1                                                                 
  \end{pmatrix},                                                            
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  where (f_x, f_y) are the focal lengths measured in pixels, and ((c_x,     
  c_y)) is the principal point, typically close to the image centre.        
  Together, these parameters determine the camera’s field of view and pixel 
  scaling.                                                                  
                                                                            
  The rotation matrix (\mathbf{R} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)) and translation       
  vector (\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^3) define how the camera moves through  
  space. Estimating these quantities from images is known as *camera pose   
  estimation*. When comparing two images in a video sequence, one is        
  usually interested in the *relative pose* between consecutive frames.     
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 2. From Pixels to Rays and Depth                                       
                                                                            
  While projection maps 3D points to pixels, many geometric problems        
  require reasoning in the opposite direction: from pixels back into        
  three-dimensional space.                                                  
                                                                            
  ### 2.1 Pixel Back-Projection and Viewing Rays                            
                                                                            
  Each pixel in an image corresponds not to a single 3D point, but to a     
  *ray* extending from the camera into the scene. This ray represents all   
  points that would project to that pixel.                                  
                                                                            
  Given a pixel (\mathbf{p} = (u,v)), we first remove the effect of camera  
  intrinsics by computing *normalised image coordinates*:                   
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{x}_n = \mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}.                         
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  This defines a direction in the camera coordinate system. Normalising     
  this vector yields a unit ray direction                                   
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{r}(u,v) = \frac{\mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}}{|\mathbf{K}^{- 
  1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}|},                                                   
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  which lies on the unit sphere. Every visible scene point corresponding to 
  pixel ((u,v)) lies somewhere along this ray.                              
                                                                            
  ### 2.2 Depth Maps                                                        
                                                                            
  To recover a specific 3D point along a ray, an additional scalar is       
  required: *depth*. A **depth map** assigns to each pixel a positive       
  scalar (d(u,v)), representing the distance of the visible surface point   
  from the camera along the optical axis.                                   
                                                                            
  Combining a depth map with back-projection lifts the image into 3D:       
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{X}_c(u,v) = d(u,v),\mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}.             
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  Depth maps therefore provide a dense geometric description of the scene   
  from a single viewpoint. Modern systems obtain depth maps from pretrained 
  neural networks. Although depth from a single image is inherently         
  ambiguous up to a global scale, such estimates provide strong relative    
  geometric structure and are sufficient for many motion-related tasks.     
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 3. Ray-Based and Model-Agnostic Camera Representations                 
                                                                            
  The pinhole model assumes an ideal camera. Real cameras often deviate     
  from this model due to lens effects. Instead of explicitly modelling      
  these effects with additional parameters, many modern approaches adopt a  
  more general representation.                                              
                                                                            
  In a **ray-based camera model**, the camera is represented directly by    
  the mapping                                                               
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  (u,v) \mapsto \mathbf{r}(u,v),                                            
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  assigning a 3D ray direction to each pixel. This mapping can be stored as 
  a dense lookup table or predicted by a neural network. Such               
  representations are *model-agnostic*: they do not assume a particular     
  lens type and apply equally to perspective, wide-angle, or fisheye        
  cameras.                                                                  
                                                                            
  Because ray directions lie on the surface of a sphere, care must be taken 
  when predicting them. A common strategy is to parameterise rays using     
  angular coordinates or to predict them in a local tangent plane and map   
  them back to the sphere, ensuring geometric consistency.                  
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 4. Optical Flow as Dense Correspondence                                
                                                                            
  When a camera moves, the same 3D scene point generally appears at         
  different pixel locations in consecutive images. **Optical flow**         
  captures this apparent motion.                                            
                                                                            
  Formally, optical flow is a dense vector field                            
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{w}_{i\to j}(u,v) = (\Delta u, \Delta v),                          
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  which maps each pixel in frame (i) to its corresponding location in frame 
  (j). Optical flow provides pixel-level correspondences across frames and  
  serves as a crucial geometric cue for estimating camera motion.           
                                                                            
  In this thesis, optical flow is treated as an observed quantity obtained  
  from a pretrained model. Given depth and camera motion, one can predict   
  how pixels *should* move between frames. Comparing this predicted motion  
  with observed optical flow yields a powerful self-supervised training     
  signal, which is described in later sections.

  ## 5. Multi-View Geometry and Camera Motion                               
                                                                            
  The concepts introduced so far describe geometry from a single viewpoint. 
  When multiple images of the same scene are available, additional          
  geometric constraints arise from the fact that the scene itself is static 
  while the camera moves. These constraints form the basis of *multi-view   
  geometry*.                                                                
                                                                            
  ### 5.1 Relative Camera Motion                                            
                                                                            
  Consider two images captured at different time steps, indexed by (i) and  
  (j). The camera undergoes a rigid motion between these frames, described  
  by a relative rotation (\mathbf{R}*{i\to j}) and translation              
  (\mathbf{t}*{i\to j}).                                                    
                                                                            
  A 3D point observed in frame (i) at pixel ((u,v)) with depth (d_i(u,v))   
  can be reconstructed in the camera coordinate system of frame (i) as      
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{X}_i = d_i(u,v),\mathbf{K}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}.                
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  Applying the relative motion maps this point into the coordinate system   
  of frame (j):                                                             
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{X}*j = \mathbf{R}*{i\to j},\mathbf{X}*i + \mathbf{t}*{i\to j}.    
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  Projecting (\mathbf{X}_j) back into the image plane of frame (j) yields   
  the pixel location where the same scene point should appear. This         
  forward–backward mapping provides a direct link between depth, camera     
  motion, and pixel correspondences.                                        
                                                                            
  ---                                                                       
                                                                            
  ## 6. Geometric Consistency Across Views                                  
                                                                            
  Multi-view geometry enables the formulation of *consistency constraints*  
  that must hold if the estimated geometry and motion are correct. These    
  constraints are central to learning-based approaches that do not rely on  
  explicit ground-truth labels.                                             
                                                                            
  ### 6.1 View Synthesis and Reprojection                                   
                                                                            
  Given an image from frame (i), its depth map, and the relative camera     
  motion to frame (j), one can synthesise an approximation of frame (j) by: 
                                                                            
  1. Lifting pixels from frame (i) into 3D using depth                      
  2. Transforming the points using the relative pose                        
  3. Projecting them into the image plane of frame (j)                      
                                                                            
  This process, known as *view synthesis*, produces a warped image          
  (\hat{I}_j). If the depth and pose estimates are accurate, (\hat{I}_j)    
  should closely resemble the true image (I_j).                             
                                                                            
  The discrepancy between the synthesised and observed images is measured   
  using a *photometric error*, typically based on pixel-wise intensity      
  differences or perceptual similarity metrics. Minimising this error       
  encourages geometrically consistent predictions.                          
                                                                            
  ### 6.2 Handling Occlusions and Visibility                                
                                                                            
  Not all pixels can be reliably compared across views. Some scene points   
  may become occluded, move outside the field of view, or violate modelling 
  assumptions such as constant illumination.                                
                                                                            
  To address this, modern systems employ visibility masks, forward–backward 
  consistency checks, or robust loss functions that reduce the influence    
  of unreliable regions. These mechanisms ensure that learning is driven    
  primarily by valid geometric correspondences.

  ## 7. Optical Flow as a Geometric Constraint                              
                                                                            
  While optical flow was previously introduced as an observed               
  correspondence field, it can also be derived from geometry. Given depth   
  and camera motion, the reprojection process implicitly defines a          
  predicted optical flow field.                                             
                                                                            
  Let (\mathbf{p}_i) be a pixel in frame (i) and (\mathbf{p}'_j) its        
  reprojected location in frame (j). The predicted flow is                  
                                                                            
  [                                                                         
  \mathbf{w}^{\text{geo}}_{i\to j}(u,v) = \mathbf{p}'_j - \mathbf{p}_i.     
  ]                                                                         
                                                                            
  Comparing this geometric flow with the observed optical flow provides an  
  additional consistency signal. Agreement indicates that depth and pose    
  are mutually consistent, while disagreement reveals geometric errors.

  ## 8. Self-Supervised Learning via Geometric Constraints                  
                                                                            
  The key advantage of the geometric relationships described above is that  
  they do not require explicit ground-truth annotations. Instead,           
  supervision arises directly from the physical structure of image          
  formation.                                                                
                                                                            
  ### 8.1 Learning Objectives                                               
                                                                            
  In a self-supervised framework, neural networks are trained to predict    
  quantities such as depth, camera motion, or ray directions by minimising  
  losses derived from:                                                      
                                                                            
  * Photometric reprojection error between synthesised and observed views   
  * Consistency between geometric and observed optical flow                 
  * Smoothness priors on depth and motion fields                            
                                                                            
  These objectives are grounded in geometry rather than semantic labels,    
  making them broadly applicable across datasets and camera configurations. 
                                                                            
  ### 8.2 Scale Ambiguity and Normalisation                                 
                                                                            
  A fundamental limitation of monocular geometry is *scale ambiguity*:      
  depth and translation can only be recovered up to a common scale factor.  
  In practice, this ambiguity is handled by normalising predicted           
  quantities or fixing scale during training.                               
                                                                            
  Although absolute scale is not recovered, relative depth structure and    
  motion direction are sufficient for tasks such as visual odometry and     
  camera calibration.

  ## 7. Robust Estimation with RANSAC

  When fitting geometric models to noisy data with outliers, standard
  least-squares methods can fail catastrophically. **RANSAC (Random Sample 
  Consensus)** provides a robust alternative widely used in computer vision.

  ### 7.1 The RANSAC Algorithm

  RANSAC fits a model by:
  1. Randomly sampling a minimal subset of data points
  2. Computing a candidate model from this subset
  3. Counting how many data points agree with the model (inliers)
  4. Repeating steps 1-3 many times
  5. Selecting the model with the most inliers

  The final model is refined using all identified inliers, typically via
  least-squares optimization.

  ### 7.2 RANSAC in Camera Calibration

  In camera calibration from predicted rays, RANSAC identifies which ray
  predictions are geometrically consistent with a pinhole camera model. Outliers
   may arise from:
  - Errors in visual feature extraction
  - Violations of model assumptions (e.g., lens distortion)
  - Occlusions or dynamic objects

  RANSAC selects the largest subset of rays that fit a pinhole model within a
  tolerance threshold, then refines the calibration parameters using only these
  inliers.

  ### 7.3 Non-Differentiability Challenge

  RANSAC presents a fundamental challenge for neural network training: it is
  **non-differentiable**. The algorithm involves:
  - Random sampling (no gradients for random selection)
  - Discrete inlier/outlier classification (discontinuous)
  - Argmax over candidates (non-differentiable operation)

  Standard backpropagation cannot flow gradients through RANSAC. This motivates
  alternative training strategies when RANSAC is part of the prediction

  ### 7.4 Weighted Least Squares Refinement

  After RANSAC identifies inliers, the camera model is refined using weighted
  least squares (WLS). Given a set of observations with reliability weights, WLS
   finds parameters that minimize the weighted sum of squared errors:

  \[
  \mathbf{x}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \|\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - 
  \mathbf{b})\|^2
  \]

  where $\mathbf{W}$ is a diagonal weight matrix (typically 1.0 for inliers, 
  near-zero for outliers), $\mathbf{A}$ encodes the measurement equations, and 
  $\mathbf{b}$ contains observed values.

  The closed-form solution:
  \[
  \mathbf{x}^* = (\mathbf{A}^\top \mathbf{W}^2 \mathbf{A})^{-1} \mathbf{A}^\top
  \mathbf{W}^2 \mathbf{b}
  \]

  is differentiable with respect to $\mathbf{b}$ (even when $\mathbf{W}$ and
  $\mathbf{A}$ are held constant), which enables gradient-based optimization in
  specific configurations.

     \]

     \[
     \mathbf{X}_j = \mathbf{R}_{i\to j}\mathbf{X}_i + \mathbf{t}_{i\to j}
     \]

  3. **Project to frame $j$**:
     \[
     \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_j = \mathbf{K}\mathbf{X}_j
     \]

  4. **Compute flow**:
     \[
     \mathbf{w}_{i\to j}^{\text{geo}}(u,v) = \mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_i
     \]

  This **geometrically induced flow** depends on depth, camera motion, and
  intrinsics.

   like UniMatch) provides a self-supervised training signal:

  \[
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{flow}} = \|\mathbf{w}_{i\to j}^{\text{geo}} -
  \mathbf{w}_{i\to j}^{\text{obs}}\|
  \]

  When depth and camera parameters are predicted by neural networks, minimizing 
  this loss encourages geometrically consistent predictions without requiring 
  ground-truth labels. Mismatches indicate errors in predicted depth, pose, or 
  intrinsics.

  ### 3.2 Tangent-Space Parameterization

  Ray directions lie on the unit sphere, which poses challenges for neural
  network regression (unbounded tangent vectors, gimbal lock). A common approach
   represents rays in tangent space relative to the optical axis:

  For a ray $\mathbf{r} = (r_x, r_y, r_z)$, the tangent coordinates are:
  \[
  \mathbf{t} = (r_x/r_z, r_y/r_z)
  \]

  These coordinates directly relate to field-of-view angles and can be bounded, 
  regularized, and predicted more stably than full 3D vectors. Conversion back 
  to unit rays uses the exponential map:
  \[
  \mathbf{r} = \frac{(t_x, t_y, 1)}{\|(t_x, t_y, 1)\|}
  \]

  This parameterization is particularly useful when learning ray predictions 
  with neural networks.

  ### 9.3 Neural Network Architectures for Geometry

  Modern geometric vision systems combine specialized neural architectures:

  **Vision Transformers (ViT)**: Process images as sequences of patches using
  self-attention mechanisms, enabling global context aggregation. Pretrained
  models like DINOv2 learn rich visual features applicable to geometric tasks.

  **Feature Aggregation**: When multiple frames are available, their
  representations can be combined using:
  - **Scalar aggregation**: Averaging final predictions (e.g., focal lengths)
  across frames
  - **Feature aggregation**: Combining intermediate representations before final
   prediction, preserving richer geometric information

  **Dense Prediction**: Tasks like depth or ray prediction require pixel-level
  outputs. Architectures like DPT (Dense Prediction Transformer) upsample and
  fuse multi-scale features to produce dense spatial predictions.

  The choice of aggregation level and architecture affects what geometric
  information is preserved during multi-frame processing.
